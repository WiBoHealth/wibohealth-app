Diabetes & Metabolic Health

Gestational Diabetes Guide: Normal Blood Sugar Levels, Foods to Eat and Avoid, and Your Day Step by Step

By Dr. Wassim Habib

Published 20 October 2026

Take a breath: you and your baby are fine, and the answer starts on your plate

The first thing most mothers feel when they hear "your pregnancy blood sugar is high" is fear and guilt: "Did I eat too many sweets? Have I hurt my baby?"

Here is the medical truth we want you to hold on to right away: gestational diabetes is not your fault, and it does not mean your baby has diabetes. It is simply your body telling you that the powerful pregnancy hormones are putting a temporary load on how you handle sugar. With a few simple, smart food steps you will get through this stage safely and in great health, for you and for your baby.

1. What is really happening in your body? (The secret is in the placenta's hormones)

The placenta is not just a way to feed your baby; it is a powerful hormone factory. In the second half of pregnancy (especially around the fifth and sixth months), the placenta releases hormones with one goal: "get as much energy and food as possible to the baby so it can grow".

To do that, these hormones make your cells more resistant to insulin (they leave sugar in the bloodstream so it can pass to the baby). Normally your pancreas steps in and releases extra insulin to balance things out. But when the pancreas falls a little behind this high demand, blood sugar rises, and that is what we call gestational diabetes.

👶 How does it affect the baby?

If the mother's blood sugar stays high, the extra sugar passes to the baby. The baby's small pancreas then has to make extra insulin to store that sugar as fat, which can make the baby large at birth. That is why managing what you eat is the strongest shield for your baby's healthy, balanced growth.

2. Gestational diabetes chart: what are the normal levels?

When you check your sugar with a home meter, there are 3 golden numbers to remember:

< 95 mg/dL

Fasting

As soon as you wake up, before breakfast. In mmol/L (Europe): below 5.3 mmol/L.

< 140 mg/dL

1 hour after a meal

In mmol/L (Europe): below 7.8 mmol/L.

< 120 mg/dL

2 hours after a meal

In mmol/L (Europe): below 6.7 mmol/L.

Why is fasting sugar sometimes high?

In the early morning hours your liver naturally releases glucose to give you energy to wake up (the dawn phenomenon). If your morning sugar runs high, the answer is not to starve yourself at night, but to have a smart bedtime snack (such as a spoon of pure peanut butter or a handful of walnuts) to steady the liver's signals, and to share your morning numbers with your doctor.

3. Gestational diabetes food list: the smart traffic-light system

Instead of harsh restriction, the medical secret is to "flatten the glucose curve": eat meals that are absorbed slowly and calmly and prevent sharp spikes.

🟢 Green light (eat freely and safely: the base of your plate)
  • Lean proteins: boiled eggs or an omelette, grilled chicken breast, and safe, well-cooked fish (such as salmon, rich in omega-3 fats for your baby's brain).
  • Healthy fats: extra virgin olive oil, avocado, walnuts and raw almonds.
  • Leafy greens and fibre: spinach, courgette, cucumber, broccoli and lettuce. (Fibre works like a sponge in your gut, soaking up sugar so it enters the bloodstream very slowly.)
🟡 Yellow light (helpful, but in smart portions)
  • Complex carbohydrates: quinoa, whole rolled oats (not instant), and naturally fermented whole-grain sourdough bread.
  • Insulin-friendly fruit: blueberries, strawberries, green apple. (Golden tip: never eat fruit on its own; always add a few almonds or a spoon of Greek yogurt to put the brakes on sugar absorption.)
🔴 Red light (avoid to keep your numbers stable)
  • Juices (even natural and freshly squeezed): juice is concentrated sugar stripped of its fibre and sends blood sugar up like an arrow within minutes. Swap it for whole fruit and a glass of water.
  • White bakery products and starches: white bread, pastries, pies and sweetened breakfast cereals.
  • Hidden sugars: maltodextrin, modified starch and corn syrup, ingredients often added to "sugar-free" products that still raise blood sugar sharply!

5. Your balanced day: a realistic, filling meal plan

  • Breakfast: two eggs cooked in olive oil with avocado slices and leafy greens (rocket and cucumber), plus a quarter of a whole-grain loaf.
  • Morning snack: a small handful of walnuts with half a cup of fresh berries.
  • Lunch: a big colourful salad with olive oil and lemon (eat it first), a piece of grilled fish or chicken, and 3 tablespoons of cooked quinoa or brown rice.
  • Afternoon snack: cucumber and carrot sticks with two tablespoons of hummus with tahini or pasteurised cottage cheese.
  • Dinner: a warm broccoli and courgette soup with sliced chicken breast, gentle on the stomach and helps prevent night-time heartburn.
  • Bedtime snack: a thin apple slice spread with a teaspoon of 100% natural peanut butter.

6. Smart clinical tricks that steady your sugar without restriction

  1. Eat your plate in order: start with vegetables and salad, then protein and fat, and leave the starch for last. This simple order noticeably softens the sugar spike after a meal!
  2. The magic 10-minute walk after eating: when you walk gently after a meal, your leg muscles open their doors to take sugar straight from the blood and burn it as energy, without asking the pancreas for extra insulin.
  3. Water is your best friend: make water your main drink instead of juice and sweetened drinks. In pregnancy you need about 2.3 litres of fluid a day (from water and food together), which keeps you hydrated without any added sugar.

7. Questions that matter to you, answered from clinical practice

Does a gestational diabetes diagnosis mean I will definitely need insulin injections?

Not at all! About 70 to 85% of pregnant women control their sugar successfully and safely with food changes and light walking alone. Insulin is only used when the numbers stay stubborn, to protect your baby; it is a very safe medicine and does not cross the placenta to the baby.

Will the diabetes stay after the birth?

In more than 90% of cases, blood sugar returns completely to normal once the placenta is delivered. Your doctor will ask for a simple test 4 to 12 weeks after the birth to be sure, and then a check every 1 to 3 years, because gestational diabetes raises the chance of type 2 diabetes later in life. Keeping the healthy eating habits that protected you and your baby is the best prevention.
